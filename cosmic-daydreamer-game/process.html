HALLUCINATION / INTERACTIVE GAME FIRST DRAFT

A little world.
A complete journey.

Cosmic Daydreamer turns Nova’s curiosity into a playable 3D adventure: approach a dream, reveal its impossible life, and collect the fragment it leaves behind.

Play the first draft ↗

From proposal to playable behavior

Lana’s Week 1 proposal established the sleeping planet, five fragments, and a relaxed experience without losing. Her Week 2 request changes the environment from 2D to 3D. This draft keeps one interaction—explore a nearby dream—across three chapters. Stage transitions wait for the player; no countdown forces the story forward.

01 / NOTICE · 1 FRAGMENT

The cloud that dreamed

At entry: the planet appears quiet. Action: approach and explore the cloud. Response: it becomes a whale and reveals a fragment. Understanding: unusual objects are invitations.

02 / FOLLOW · 2 FRAGMENTS

The wandering meadow

At entry: dreams can change ordinary things. Action: explore the pebble and flower in either order. Response: the rock walks; the blossom grows enormous. Understanding: the same action rewards curiosity in different places.

03 / DISCOVER · 2 FRAGMENTS

A dream within a dream

At entry: transformations can show a route. Action: explore the dancing star, collect its fragment, enter the moon door, and find the hidden star. Response: the surroundings become a separate garden. Understanding: a small doorway can contain an entire dream.

Visual and sound direction

The supplied images suggest impossible thresholds, luminous gardens, small planets, and soft celestial color. The draft translates those observations into rounded 3D forms, a branching light tree, a whale-cloud, a pink doorway, and gold collectibles. These are original procedural models; the reference images are not used as game textures.

A fixed angled camera keeps movement readable. Dream labels, text hints, and a permanent counter support discovery without relying on color or sound alone. Gentle guidance can be disabled; reduced motion removes bobbing and drifting effects. A quiet synthesized chord loop and short bell cues are optional and start only after a player action.

A design question carried forward

Lana’s earlier challenge, condensed: Does surprise always feel relaxing? The game should remain cozy, and players do not all enjoy the same amount of unpredictability.

Implementation response: ambient events vary, but controls and progression remain dependable. Fragments do not expire, discoveries can be revisited, guidance is optional, and every chapter waits for the player. This applies the actual Week 1 discussion to the first draft; it is not a new player-test transcript.

Development checks and one revision

Rules checks completed: a local automated test covered opening, gated discoveries, duplicate prevention, pause/resume, both chapter transitions, entering and leaving the dream garden, victory, free wandering, and full restart.

Browser observations: the opening and 3D island rendered without console errors, and the running preview reached the five-fragment ending. Restart returned the counter to 0 / 5 and the opening screen. A 390-pixel browser viewport had no horizontal overflow. Physical phone testing remains a next step.

Observed visual issue → revision: the first island preview was pale and lacked a central focal point. Lighting intensity was reduced, sky colors deepened, and a luminous branching dream tree added. A final rules check also fixed re-entering the moon door while wandering after the ending. These are development observations, not claims about classmates’ feedback.

What to test next

  • Ask first-time players to explain the goal without coaching. Observe whether reveal → collect is clear.
  • Compare guidance on and off. Does the portal feel discoverable without making the hidden star too obvious?
  • Check performance on classmates’ phones and laptops, sound balance, and whether the pace feels cozy.
  • Refine the landscape, camera framing, creature motion, and sound from observed feedback. This first draft uses one island and one small dream garden; progress is not saved between visits.

Lesson, credits, and authorship

The process follows the professor’s Build Your Web Game Demo: define a brief, events and reset rules, establish a visual guide, build a playable loop, add three stages, test, and publish. The eight lessons’ descriptions, example prompts, and subtitled video frames were reviewed during development.
